Phase 02 · 5–7 hours

Pinch & pressure.

A pinch pot is not a beginner’s placeholder. It is a direct record of force. Learn to feel thickness, redirect pressure, and make three related forms with intention.

Begin the pressure labSee the project

Before you begin

From “squeeze clay” to controlled deformation.

Prerequisite

Complete Phase 1 or be able to identify workable, soft leather-hard, and firm leather-hard clay. Keep three small test pieces available for comparison.

By the end

You can create even walls, hold or change a profile deliberately, preserve a rim, and explain how each touch altered the form.

Deliverables

A pressure ladder, a three-profile study, a gesture cup, and a coherent three-form set with process notes.

Clay preparation gateUse only studio-approved clay. Check for foreign material—especially plaster contamination—and portion with a wire pulled away from your body and other people. Under local instruction, use an appropriate wedging method when clay needs its moisture and consistency homogenized. Wedging can improve workability and redistribute larger voids; it does not make damp ware safe to fire. Complete, even drying and an appropriate firing schedule control steam risk; air alone is not the standard explanation for breakage.
02.1

Pressure has direction.

Why does one pinch thin the wall while another only moves the whole pot?

Mental model

Support → compress → move

Your outside finger supports the wall; the inside thumb compresses clay against it; both move together. Pressure without support stretches and weakens. Support without movement leaves dents.

Touch cues

Read thickness indirectly

Rotate the form instead of chasing one spot. Compare the drag and flex between opposite fingers. A thin area often feels quick and flexible while a thick area feels slow and resistant; perceived temperature is not a reliable thickness cue. Confirm with finger spacing, calipers where suitable, backlighting for thin translucent sections, or sacrificial sectioning. Verify by sectioning one practice pot.

Lab · Pressure ladder + autopsy60–75 min
  1. Make four equal 120 g balls from the same clay. Mark them A–D.
  2. Use four pressure levels: barely a trace, light compression, firm controlled compression, and deliberately excessive force.
  3. Keep rhythm, rotation, and target height constant. Record cracks, dents, wall movement, and fatigue.
  4. Cut each pot vertically with wire. Compare rim, shoulder, base transition, and wall thickness. Sketch the profiles.
  5. Remake the best result using fewer, slower passes. Your target is not a number—it is a continuous, supported wall.

Clay bodies and hand sizes differ. Avoid universal wall-thickness targets; judge evenness, structural support, intended scale, and firing requirements.

Troubleshooting by evidence

EvidenceLikely causeNext action
Thin band below rimThumb stayed in one height zoneStart each circuit lower; move both hands upward together.
Cracked rimRim drying or being pulled outwardCompress between finger and thumb; cover between rounds.
Heavy base ledgePinching began too highOpen a wider interior floor and compress the floor-wall transition.
Random dentsPressure before supportPlace the outside finger first, then meet it from inside.
02.2

A profile is a sequence of decisions.

Silhouette carries mass and direction. Think in three zones: foot/base, belly/transition, and rim/ending. Change one zone at a time so you can attribute the result.

Study · One mass, three profiles90 min

Start with three equal clay masses and the same opening diameter.

Contained

Full belly, inward shoulder, compressed rim. The eye is held inside.

Open

Gentle outward wall, broad mouth, quiet rim. The form offers its interior.

Lifted

Narrower base, rising wall, slight outward rim. The profile carries upward energy.

Photograph each at eye level against the same background. Trace its contour. Annotate where you pushed, supported, compressed, or rested the clay.

Transfer challengeWithout adding clay, change the emotional reading of one form from stable to alert. Write which two profile decisions created the change.
02.3

Keep evidence of the hand.

Gesture is not damage

Useful marks reinforce direction, rhythm, or grip. Accidental marks interrupt them. Choose a visual rule—such as upward sweeps, paired thumb marks, or a quiet exterior—and repeat it consistently.

Compression still matters

A lively surface can be structurally sound. Compress cracks closed while clay is responsive; do not merely smear wet slip over a weak split.

Make · Gesture cup60–90 min
  1. Make a cup that fits your hand and holds roughly 250–350 ml.
  2. Define one gesture rule and one “quiet zone” where touch recedes.
  3. Test grip with dry hands. Check for sharp rims, unstable foot, and uncomfortable pressure points.
  4. Rotate it under side light. Remove only marks that contradict your rule.
Retrieval: explain without looking
  1. What are the three parts of the pressure model?
  2. Why is sectioning a practice pot useful?
  3. How can two forms made from equal clay feel visually different?
Suggested answers

Support, compression, and coordinated movement; sectioning reveals thickness hidden from touch; profile, mass distribution, opening, rim, and directional gesture change the reading.

02.4

Project: a family of three.

Integrated project · 2–3 hours

Three related vessels, three distinct characters.

Use the same clay mass and one shared gesture rule. Vary containment, lift, or openness. The set should look related without becoming copies.

  1. Sketch six silhouettes at thumbnail size. Circle three that share a visual grammar.
  2. Make all three in one session, alternating between them every circuit to equalize moisture and attention.
  3. At soft leather hard, compare from eye level and overhead. Refine feet and rims without erasing the making process.
  4. Photograph, measure, and write a 100-word account: what stayed constant, what varied, and what you would remake.
Drying: loosely cover and equalize moisture. Drying time depends on clay, thickness, humidity, airflow, and form. Progress when evidence—not a fixed timetable—supports it.

Inspiration · look comparatively

Clay records force in different ways.

Compare a hand-built bowl whose rim becomes a sculptural stage, pressure through a mold, and a bowl deliberately compressed off-axis. Describe the evidence before naming the method.

Knowledge check · 3 questions

Pause. Predict. Diagnose.

Choose one answer for each situation. Open a hint when needed; explanations appear after you check all three.

Exit questions

  1. Predict what will happen if you repeatedly pinch only at the rim.
  2. How would you make a form feel grounded without adding clay?
  3. Which evidence would distinguish a weak seam-like split from a surface scratch?
